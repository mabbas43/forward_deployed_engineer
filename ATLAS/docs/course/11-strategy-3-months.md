# Strategy — master ATLAS in 3 months (signal over noise)

Jobs' rule was never "do less." It was: decide what the *one* thing is, then say no to everything that competes with it, including good things. Here's the strategy.

## 1. Name the signal

The signal is one sentence: **"By December 17 I have a deployed, evaluated, governed agent system I can defend in a 90-minute interview, plus nine public write-ups."** Everything is measured against that. Not "learn LLMOps." Not "get better at agents." A thing that exists, with a URL.

## 2. Kill the noise (this is the hard part)

You're running five learning tracks plus freelancing. That's noise dressed as diligence. For 13 weeks:

- **Ed Donner's course, the Python roadmap, math:** paused. Not reduced — paused. ATLAS *is* advanced Python and *is* production AI; you'll learn more Python building a circuit breaker than reading about the GIL. Math for transformers has zero bearing on whether ATLAS ships.
- **English:** keep it, 1 hr/day, because it serves the same goal (Germany). But make the writing practice *be* the phase write-ups.
- **Freelancing:** don't stop looking, but only pitch work that overlaps a phase (a RAG bot, an MCP integration). A client project that's Phase 2 work is signal. A WordPress chatbot is noise.
- **New tools, new frameworks, new courses that appear on your feed:** ignore for 13 weeks. If it's still important in December, it'll still be there.

The rule: if a task doesn't move a gate sentence closer to true, it doesn't get hours this quarter.

## 3. Time: 4 hours of deep work beats 15 hours of activity

You said 15 hrs/day available. Use 6, not 15. Split:

- **Block A (3 hrs, morning, no phone):** build. Lab code only. This is the only block that produces the artifact.
- **Block B (2 hrs):** resources for the *current* phase only. Never read ahead. Reading Phase 7 material during Phase 2 feels productive and is procrastination.
- **Block C (1 hr):** write-up, notes, English.

Six focused hours × 6 days = 36 hrs/week. That beats the 30-hr calendar with margin. The other 9 hours are for rest, freelance pitching, and life — and rest is not optional, it's what makes Block A work.

## 4. Sequence with a bias to the hard middle

The 14-week calendar is right but the risk isn't evenly spread. Phases 1–4 you'll finish fast — you've done most of it before. **Phases 5–7 are where you're weak and where the market pays.** So:

- Weeks 1–3: Phases 1–4 compressed. Move fast, don't gold-plate. If you already have a hybrid retriever from an old project, reuse it; measure it, don't rebuild it.
- Weeks 4–10: Phases 5, 6, 7 at full two weeks each. No compression here. These are the phases whose write-ups get you interviews.
- Weeks 11–12: Phase 8. You know AWS; this is about the shape, not the buttons.
- Week 13: capstone, demo video, ship.

## 5. The gate is a real gate

Jobs shipped nothing that wasn't finished. The temptation will be to say "Phase 3 is basically done" and move on. It isn't done until the checkpoint task is committed and the gate sentence is true without lying. A half-done Phase 6 is worth nothing in a German interview; a fully-done one is worth the job. Faking gates is the single most common way this plan fails.

## 6. Ship in public every Friday, no exceptions

Nine write-ups, nine Fridays. Bad and published beats perfect and private. The write-ups are the product as much as the code — they're what recruiters actually see. Write them in the English hour.

## 7. One review checkpoint, week 7

Halfway, ask three questions: Am I on Phase 5 or 6? Have I published 5 write-ups? Have I said no to something I wanted to do? If any answer is no, you're not off track on skill — you're off track on focus, and the fix is cutting more, not working more.

---

If you want it in one line: **six deep hours, one project, no new inputs, publish every Friday, and every gate is real.** That's the whole strategy. The course does the rest.
